June 2, 2026

Foundation

The most important part of this foundation is the gray plate. With holes in a grid, the design is meant to be as modular as possible, allowing parts like the blue beams shown to be placed anywhere. Heatset brass threaded inserts were used for a secure fit on all load-bearing connective parts.

Building process of chassis

Also pictured here are the 3D printed wheels and treads made. The wheel is PLA, but the custom treads are TPU. After little testing, these were determined to be too slippy and real rubber treads were then ordered.

June 8, 2026

Installing first electronics

With the chassis starting to take form, I started to think about where everything would fit on the inside. To start out, a 3D printed "mesa" was made to mount a large breadboard.

This allowed the IMU to be mounted flat along with make the connections back the the microcontroller.

June 12, 2026

Low Voltage Cutoff

Without knowing many characteristics of the battery, safety was one of the highest priorities before using it on the robot. In addition to a 5 Amp fuse and power switch, this low voltage cutoff circuitry provides safety the battery itself. Split onto two boards, they share 24V, GND, and Signal. The first board has a 5V voltage regulator, a comparator, and flip flop. It outputs a high signal when there is a voltage input greater than 24V.

The signal from the first board is taken by the second board and drives a transistor to activate a relay and indicator LED. When the supply voltage falls under 18.5V, the signal latches low and cannot go high again without restart.

LVCO boards

This design does not use any hysteris, which would have been possible, but from a safety standpoint if the battery drops below 18.5V, the desired logic is to not allow any more power distribution at all.

The boards were then mounted on either side of the battery with more M3 screws and heatset inserts, with their connecting harness bridging over the battery.

August 6, 2026

Getting a Head

It was finally time to make the head, and thankfully there were already 4 brass threaded inserts surrounding the battery from earlier which made mounting easy. Extra lights were needed here as well of course. Data, power, and ground, are daisy chained from the underglow up to the left eye, then the right eye, then the mouth.

August 15, 2026

3D Printed Decal

The controller was feeling like it had a lot of dead space. Thankfully, with minimal effort in Fusion I was able to insert an image of GanzBot and trace some key features.

Thanks to the ease of the AMS on my Bambu A1, this flush detail was easy to get right and makes the controller's purpose unmistakable.

While not super necessary, this was actually the second print of the shell after a few dimensions were changed for the cutouts of the power switch and screen.

August 16, 2026

Internal Controller Wiring + Batteries

With code tested and the housing close to being ready to close up, the last part of the process was to add a battery pack and clean up all the wiring.

A standard 4 slot AA battery pack was used, and the fourth slot was just shorted by soldering a wire across it. This deliverys ~4.5V (1.5 per battery), which is within operating limits of the pico 2.

The extra length in the wires allow for easy access the all internal components when removing the outer screws for maintenance. Next up is figuring out what to do with these buttons.
